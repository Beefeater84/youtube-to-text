## Understanding CV Importance

If you've been applying for jobs and hearing nothing back, the problem is probably your CV. I don't say that to be harsh; I say it because it's the bit most people get wrong. No one really teaches you how to write a good CV, and there's so much conflicting advice out there. Some people say keep it to one page, while others say it needs to be super detailed. You end up throwing everything on it, hoping something sticks, and then wondering why it's not working.

Here's the truth: recruiters don't actually read your CV. We scan it for six seconds, that's all it gets, maybe ten if we're feeling generous. In those few seconds, we're trying to figure out one thing: do you look like someone who could do this job? That's it. If we can't see that straight away, we move on. It's not personal; it's just time. There might be a hundred other CVs sitting in the same inbox.

<!-- t:50 -->
## Key Elements Recruiters Look For

When I say six seconds, I mean it literally. We open your CV, our eyes go straight to the top third of the page, and we're looking for a few key things: who are you, what do you do, where are you based, what's your most recent job, and does it match what we're hiring for? If we can tick those boxes fast, then great, we'll keep reading. But if it's unclear, too vague, or buried under a load of waffle, then it's going in the no pile. Not because you're not qualified, but because it's just not obvious.

At the top, we're looking for your name, obviously, but also your location—not your full address, just your city or town. If your CV doesn't say where you live, we've got no idea whether you're local to the job or not. We're not going to guess or check LinkedIn; we're just moving on to the next one. Then we check your contact details: phone number, email, LinkedIn profile if you've got one. That's all fine, but what's missing from most CVs is a job title. You need to tell me what you are right at the top under your name. One line: are you a finance manager, a marketing assistant, a customer service representative? Just tell me.

<!-- t:125 -->
## Crafting a Professional Summary

Then we move down to your professional summary, and this is where a lot of people go wrong. Most summaries are just fluff—long paragraphs that don't actually say anything. Phrases like 'proven track record' or 'results-driven individual' are just noise. What I want to see is who you are, what you do, what you've achieved, and what kind of role you're looking for, all in about four lines max.

For example, 'Experienced sales manager with eight years in B2B environments, consistently hitting targets and growing revenue. Led teams of up to ten, managed key accounts, and implemented new CRM processes. Now looking for a leadership role in a growth-focused company.' That's clear. I know what you are, what you've done, and what you want.

<!-- t:171 -->
## Detailing Work Experience

Next, I'm jumping straight to your most recent job: company name, job title, dates—that's all standard. What I want to see is clarity. I want to see what you actually did in that job, and ideally, I want to see some results. Most people just list tasks: 'responsible for this,' 'assisted with that.' That's fine, but it doesn't tell me how well you did it. If you implemented something that saved time, brought in more business, reduced errors, or improved a process, tell me that and use numbers if you can. If you say you reduced call waiting times by 20%, that tells me a lot more than just 'answered calls.'

A good rule of thumb is this: if I took your name off your CV and showed it to someone, would it read like a job description, or would it show me what kind of impact you had in the role? If it's just a list of duties, it could be anyone's. But if you talk about what changed because you were in that role, then it's yours—that's what makes it stand out.

## Making Your CV Readable

Another thing to keep in mind: we're not reading every word. We're skimming, so your CV needs to be easy to read. Use a simple layout—one column, clear headings, bullet points, and white space. Don't cram everything in; make it scannable. Please don't use two columns; it looks neat but is harder to follow. We read left to right, so don't make me work to find the info I need because I won't. I'll just skip it.

Now that you understand how quickly recruiters skim your CV, let's talk about how to actually fix it. This isn't about rewriting your whole life story; it's about making the good stuff easier to find. You want your CV to work like a signpost, pointing people straight to the information that matters.

<!-- t:266 -->
## Structuring Your CV Effectively

The first thing to get right is the structure. Your CV needs to follow a layout that makes sense and reads naturally. That usually means starting with your name and contact info at the top, then straight into your job title. That line under your name is crucial; don't leave it blank. Tell us who you are in one sentence. Think of it like the headline of your story. If you're a project manager, say that. If you're a junior designer, say that. Keep it simple and relevant to the job you're going for.

Then we move into your professional summary, where you can really start pulling the reader in. Think about what this specific employer is likely looking for and show them you've got it. If the job is all about stakeholder management and delivering under pressure, you need to be talking about those things. This doesn't mean lying or forcing it; it just means leading with the right parts of your experience.

<!-- t:355 -->
## Highlighting Skills and Experience

Under the summary, I recommend adding a short skills section—ten bullet points max. This is your chance to get some keywords on the page because, let's be honest, recruiters sometimes search CVs by keyword, especially in internal systems. But don't just throw in buzzwords; include actual skills you use: tools, software, methodologies, languages—whatever's relevant. Only include things you'd be happy to talk about in an interview.

Now we get into the main part: your experience. This is where it all falls apart for most people. You don't need to write an essay for every job you've ever had; just follow a clear pattern: job title, company name, date you worked there, and then underneath, a short paragraph about the role, followed by a few bullet points that focus on outcomes.

<!-- t:439 -->
## Addressing Job Gaps and Education

Let's quickly talk about job gaps because people get nervous about those. First of all, gaps are normal, especially now. Life happens: redundancy, burnout, caring responsibilities, travel, study, visa issues—it's all fine. But don't ignore it; put something on the CV to explain the gap, even if it's just a one-liner: 'career break due to redundancy, actively seeking new opportunities.' That's enough; you don't need to over-explain.

For your education section, keep it brief: your degree, your A-levels, or whatever your highest qualification is. If you've done any professional certifications that are actually relevant, stick them in here, but don't go all the way back to every course you've ever taken unless it adds value. No one's hiring you because you did a half-day Excel course in 2014.

<!-- t:495 -->
## Final Tips for a Strong CV

That's really it: contact information, clear job title, short summary, skills, experience, education. You don't need hobbies unless they're impressive or relevant, and you don't need a references line; everyone knows they're available on request. You definitely don't need a photo. Just stick to what helps the recruiter say yes.

The real key is simplicity. The more cluttered your CV is, the harder it is for someone to see what you bring to the table. You're not trying to tell your whole story; you're just trying to get the interview. That's the job of a CV—nothing more, nothing less. You want someone to look at it and think, 'Yep, I want to talk to this person.'

## Tailoring Your CV for Job Applications

Now that you've got the structure sorted, your layout's clear, and the content's easy to skim, let's talk about this idea of tailoring. Most of the advice you hear about tailoring your CV for every single role is complete nonsense. It's well-meaning, but it's not realistic and it's not how most recruiters actually work. The truth is, if you're applying for roles in the same job family—say operations, admin, marketing, project management, sales—most of those roles are looking for the same types of skills. The job titles might vary slightly, but the expectations are pretty consistent.

Instead of spending hours rewriting your CV every time you click apply, you're better off creating one strong, clear version that's targeted to that job family as a whole. You're not tailoring to a job; you're tailoring to a type of job. Think of it like building a foundation: you create a CV that speaks directly to your skill set as, say, an HR advisor, and once you've done that properly, you don't need to keep tweaking it every five minutes.

<!-- t:683 -->
## Ensuring Clarity and Relevance

If you're applying for HR advisor roles, they're all broadly going to ask for things like employee relations, onboarding, systems like Workday or SAP, stakeholder management, maybe some basic reporting or policy work. They're not reinventing the wheel. So if your CV already speaks to those areas, you're good—that's tailored enough. The same goes for a marketing assistant; they'll all want campaign support, content updates, maybe CRM knowledge, social media scheduling—the usual stuff.

What matters is that your CV clearly reflects the type of role you're going for. Yes, you still need to get specific; you still need to make sure your summary reflects the type of work you do, that your bullet points show impact, and that the skills you've listed are relevant. But once you've done that, you don't need to keep rewriting it every time a job ad changes.

<!-- t:739 -->
## Final Thoughts on CV Preparation

If you've been sitting there trying to force your CV to match a new job ad every time, stop. You don't need to get your foundation right; make sure it's aligned to the type of roles you're going for. Once it's working—sharp, clear, and focused—you'll get responses because at that point, you're making it easy for someone to say, 'Yep, this person gets it, let's bring them in.' Not every CV is going to land; sometimes you'll still get ghosted or be up against 300 applicants, and it just comes down to timing. But if you're consistently getting nothing back and you know your experience is solid, your first move should always be to fix the CV. It's the first impression, and if that impression isn't clear, confident, and relevant, you'll never get the chance to show them what you can really do.

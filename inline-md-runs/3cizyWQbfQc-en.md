## Reevaluating Career Advice in a Changing Job Market

I want you to stop following some of the career advice you may have picked up along the way. Some of it I might have even shared with you over the years. It's not necessarily because it's bad advice. Some of it's actually still really relevant, but the job market's changed. The way that people apply for jobs has changed, and the tools that they are using to apply for those jobs has also changed. While the fundamentals of how employers assess candidates and how they select people for certain jobs really hasn't changed all that much, the amount of competition that you're dealing with for a lot of these jobs absolutely has. Some of the things that may have worked perfectly for you five or maybe ten years ago need to be approached a little differently today. In the spirit of evolving, and I think we all need to evolve in our careers, I want to walk through seven pieces of career advice that I think you should at least reconsider because following some of them the way you always have could make your job search that much harder or keep you stuck in your career longer than you need to be.

## Work Hard and Get Ahead? Not Always True

The first piece of advice is to work hard and eventually you'll get ahead. There's absolutely nothing wrong with working hard. In fact, we should be working harder in our career and we should be good at the job that we do. But I talk to people all the time who have been in the same position for five, six, seven, eight plus years, and they're frustrated because they keep getting passed over for promotions. When I ask them, 'What are you doing to get promoted?' a lot of times the answer comes to some version of, 'Well, I'm doing really good work. I'm the best person in my department. I'm taking on all these extra responsibilities. I'm doing the work of this other person already. Why won't they just notice me?' But who's gotten promoted around you? If you've been there for six or seven years and you've made it clear that you want to move up to your boss and the people above your boss, and they've promoted three other people instead of you, your company is basically telling you how they feel about you. At that point, you have to decide whether or not there's an actual future there for you because chances are your brand inside of the organization is well-established, and it's going to be almost impossible to move that needle away from it. Maybe they think you're a really strong individual contributor. Maybe they think you're dependable. Maybe you're the person they give the difficult work to because they know you're going to get it done. But they have consistently passed you over, and at least you have to consider the possibility that they simply don't see you as somebody who can operate at the next level. Doing more of the same thing for another year or three, thinking that things are going to change, probably isn't going to happen. If they're giving you specific feedback, putting you on projects, preparing you for the next level, and telling you this, introducing you to the right people in the organization, and you're actually seeing movement toward the promotion, maybe you're just not quite ready for it yet. But if none of this is happening and you're simply waiting for somebody to eventually notice how hard you're working and throw your name into the ring for you, personally, I wouldn't be building my career around that. The stakes get considerably higher the further you get in your career. If you're 35, 45, 55, and you spend another five years sitting in the wrong level waiting for an opportunity that's probably never going to come, you don't get those five years back.

## Rethinking Job Hopping for Higher Pay

The second thing I would start re-evaluating, especially in this market, is job hopping and whether it's the fastest way to make more money. The advice absolutely works. There are times where leaving for another company is the right move. But I think the risk calculation is considerably different today than maybe it was even a couple of years ago. When companies are hiring aggressively and there are a lot of good jobs available, you can afford to take more chances. If the new company doesn't work out for whatever reason, there's a better chance you can get back into the market and find something else. But it's a much different decision when companies are hiring more slowly, like they are in today's market, and there aren't as many good quality jobs available, and you've got more people competing for the ones that are. If I'm making $80,000 a year and another company comes along and offers me, say, $85,000 a year, I'm not automatically leaving a good job because somebody else waved another five grand in front of me. I'd want to know what I'm walking into. Why is the position open? How stable is the organization? What happened with the business? Who am I going to work for? What happened to the person who was sitting in the role before me? Is this actually better for my career, or am I just getting paid slightly more money? Because let's say you take the job and then three months later they decide to reorganize and eliminate your position. Now, you've just left a stable job, you're unemployed in a more difficult market, and you've got a three-month job sitting somewhere on your resume that now you have to explain. Some people say, 'Well, you can just lie about it.' But that stuff comes up in background checks, and you're not going to be able to hide it for very long. If you've done that once or twice over the period of time, it's probably not that big of a deal. But if you've done this a few times, you're going to start creating another problem. I've personally heard hiring managers look at somebody who's been in three, maybe four jobs in maybe a five-year period, and then they start creating their own story about that person. They may have had a legitimate reason for leaving every one of those jobs, but the hiring manager doesn't necessarily know that. They're going to start asking why this person keeps leaving, and they're going to challenge me, the recruiter, and say, 'Why are you sending me these candidates who can't keep a job for longer than six months?'

## The Importance of Authentic Resume Customization

Next, we have customizing your resume for every job. Yes, you absolutely should be customizing your resume for the job that you're applying to. This is a fundamental thing that I believe increases people's chances of truly conveying their fit for the roles that they're applying to. Every job is different, every person's background is different, and how you want to market yourself for the specific role needs to adjust based on the job in front of you. The problem is what people now think customizing your resume actually means. To them, it represents a lot of effort, and it represents that elephant in the room. They know that they should be doing it, but it's difficult. It's time-consuming. They don't really know how to do it. So, they do what everybody does. They head over to Reddit or to X and they find somebody who's sharing a prompt or they see some influencer talking about some generic resume prompt that they're willing to share. Then they throw their resume into chat GPT or some other resume customizing site and tell it to rewrite everything to match the job posting. The problem is that if it's easy for you to do it and somebody is sharing it at scale, everybody else is doing the same exact thing. You're not unique. Hiring teams are seeing a lot of resumes that read very closely to the job posting. They have these incredibly polished accomplishments that don't always make sense when you look at the person's actual background. Customization by itself isn't enough. It has to be based on what you've actually done. It has to be authentic. The claims you're making need to make sense for the level you're at and the amount of time you were actually in that job to perform the work and to make that impact. If you're only somewhere for three months, it's not realistic to expect that you would have transformed an entire organization in such a short amount of time.

## Due Diligence Before Major Job Moves

Once you land a good job, assuming that you're safe, you need to keep your networks active. I had coached somebody recently who had left a good stable employer for another opportunity because they dangled a little bit more money, a little bit better title. On paper, it seemed like it made sense. The problem is that she moved across the country to take the job. She wasn't even through the onboarding period when senior leadership changed, and they brought in a new person who changed all the priorities of the organization. The company reorganized and eliminated her position. Now she's living completely across the country, in a different state, doesn't have the support network, doesn't really know the market, and she left an employer where she had stability. That employer had already filled that job, so she couldn't even go back to it. Now she's scrambling to find another job in a market where not only is she unfamiliar with her local market, but the entire market as a whole is not very advantageous for a person to be able to find something quickly. There are some things you simply can't predict. Your hiring manager genuinely wants you to be there. The headcount can be approved. HR could be signed off on it. The vision for you in that role could be well aligned with the people who are there. Then something changes. In this market, I'd be doing considerably more due diligence before making a major move, especially if I'm relocating for it. Has there been leadership changes? Are there any rumors of leadership changes coming? What's the financial health of the company? Have they lost a major customer recently? Are there any reorganizations happening? You have to keep your resume current. Even if you don't need it, at least keep it current. Pay attention to what's happening around you. Pay attention to the market. Pay attention to those town hall meetings and those press releases.

## The Necessity of Self-Promotion

One of my favorites is if you're good at what you do, you shouldn't have to market yourself. I hear this one or some version of it all the time. The reason is that the company you're applying to on the open market has no clue who you are and doesn't know anything about you. I've talked to people who were considered to be some of the very best people in their organization at what they did. They had all the equity inside of their department. Everybody knew what value they brought, their expertise, their smarts, their impact. The problem is that being the best person in your department still doesn't insulate you from potential layoffs. When they get laid off and go out to the open market, they're shocked that nobody seems to understand or recognize how good they actually are. I understand why that's frustrating. You spend 10 or 15 years proving yourself to your current organization or the places that you've worked in the past, and now some random company, some random recruiter, some random hiring manager wants you to prove that you can do it all over again. But that's kind of what you have to do. You have to know how to write a resume that shows what you've actually done in a way that they care about. You have to network. You need to keep your skills relevant. As you get further into your career, you should be thinking about your professional reputation outside of the company that currently employs you.

## Quality Over Quantity in Job Applications

If you apply for enough jobs, eventually something's going to hit. This one has gotten considerably worse with all the mass applying tools. People are applying to hundreds of jobs at a time thinking that they're improving their odds because they're getting more applications out there. But applying for more jobs just the brute force mechanism of shooting your resume out to hundreds of employers does not make you more qualified for those jobs. At a company like Amazon, recruiters can see your application history inside of the company. If you've applied to a hundred different jobs, especially if they're in a short amount of time, I'm now looking at somebody who apparently wants to be a product manager, a program manager, a developer, a recruiter, a marketing manager, or whatever else they clicked on. What exactly are you qualified to do? That's what recruiters are starting to look at, and they can see that you're all over the place. Submitting more applications is not getting people hired faster. It just gets more people informally blacklisted. I realize that that's more work, but there's not really an easy way around it.

## Credentials vs. Experience in Job Market

The final one is that getting another credential will make you more marketable. I see a lot of people do this when they're stuck. They're not getting any interviews or they're not getting promoted, and then they start asking, 'Well, would a certification do it? Or should I get an MBA?' On some level, yeah, the credentials can matter. It's certainly not going to make you a weaker candidate in most cases. But a credential in and of itself isn't going to replace experience, and that's where people tend to get hung up. They get all of these credentials, but they have nothing really to back up the actual work that's behind the credential. It isn't automatically going to change how the market perceives you. Before you spend tens of thousands, maybe even hundreds of thousands of dollars trying to get ahead, personally, I would want to know that the credential is actually solving a problem that I have and the market is actually demanding it. Maybe what you need is better experience or maybe a portfolio that demonstrates the skill that you think the certification's going to replace. If the credential solves a specific requirement, then absolutely great. Go ahead and do it, but I wouldn't just sit there collecting a bunch of credentials because I don't know what else to do.

## Conclusion: Adapting Career Strategies

The point of this video isn't that all this advice has suddenly become bad. Some of it absolutely still does work. You just have to be more thoughtful about how you're applying it because the environment around how we operate in our career and our job search has certainly changed over the years. Companies are still hiring the same way; they're just using different tools and different methodologies to do it.
